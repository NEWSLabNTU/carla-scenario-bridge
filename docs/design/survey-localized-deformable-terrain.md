# Confine soil physics to zones, grip everywhere

Survey for [off-road-surfaces.md](off-road-surfaces.md), 2026-10-22: scientific terramechanics, off-road games, wet and loose surfaces, and what Isaac Sim 6.1 offers, from published sources. Nothing in it was measured here; the Isaac measurements are in off-road-surfaces.md.

Scientific terramechanics codes and off-road games handle large worlds the same way. Soil is simulated only inside bounded regions, and only near the wheels that touch it. Deformation is stored as a sparse height delta over terrain that never changes, and everything else is a cheap per-material friction and drag model. For our stack this means two layers, and the evidence supports a fairly specific design for each. The **global layer is rigid**. A georeferenced surface-state raster (surface class, wetness, water depth, loose-gravel state) drives the PhysX tire-friction table and its slip curves, and a few script-side corrections cover hydroplaning and rolling resistance. It costs almost nothing and works on any CARLA town. The **zone layer is a per-wheel Bekker-Wong-Janosi soil model of the kind in Chrono's SCM**. It runs on a sparse tile grid that is allocated only inside authored mud and sand polygons. It is coupled to the PhysX vehicle by lowering each wheel's contact plane and adding resistance forces, not by replacing PhysX. Higher-fidelity granular methods (SPH, DEM, MPM) are still **20-70x slower than real time for a single wheel** even after their own active-domain optimizations. Their role is offline calibration. Isaac Sim 6.1 has no native vehicle deformable terrain, so the zone layer is ours to build. Expect believable rather than predictive behavior: Bekker-type models typically land within **±20-30% on drawbar pull**, sinkage errors are worse, and no published model gets mud right.

## Every system that scales confines soil to small active regions

The previous study found whole-world deformable simulation infeasible. That is not a limit peculiar to Project Chrono. Every mature system, scientific or commercial, avoids it by design, and they converge on three ideas: soil exists only in authored regions; within a region it is computed only in small boxes that follow the contacting bodies; and state is stored sparsely, as changes against immutable base terrain.

Chrono shows the pattern most explicitly. SCMTerrain, its Bekker-Wong height-field model, ray-casts only inside oriented boxes attached to bodies (`AddActiveDomain`). It **stores only deformed nodes in a sparse map that "only ever grows"**, so memory follows the area actually driven over, not the patch size. `SetBoundary` stops soil forces outside an AABB, and `GetModifiedNodes`/`SetModifiedNodes` give a per-step delta stream for rendering, co-simulation or save/restore ([Chrono SCMTerrain reference](https://api.chrono.projectchrono.org/classchrono_1_1vehicle_1_1_s_c_m_terrain.html)). The HMMWV demo attaches **one 1 x 0.5 x 1 m box per wheel spindle**. The terrain docs name ray casting as SCM's dominant cost, held in check by those boxes ([Chrono terrain docs](https://api.projectchrono.org/vehicle_terrain.html); [demo source](https://github.com/projectchrono/chrono/blob/main/src/demos/vehicle/terrain/demo_VEH_SCMTerrain_WheeledVehicle.cpp)). CRM, Chrono's SPH continuum model, does the same in 3D. Particles outside "active boxes" skip neighbor search and give up their GPU memory, which yields **2.1-2.9x from active domains alone and over 7x (up to 15.9x) combined with persistent neighbor lists** ([CRM performance guide](https://api.chrono.projectchrono.org/vehicle_terrain_crm_performance.html)).

Commercial earthmoving simulators expose the same split to their users. In Vortex Studio, terrain is static unless it lies inside an authored **"Earthwork Zone"** or the larger, performance-optimized "Deformable Terrain" region; wheels have rutted such terrain since 2019b ([Vortex Earthwork Systems](https://vortexstudio.atlassian.net/wiki/spaces/VSD2408/pages/3716922665/Earthwork+Systems); [CM Labs 2019b](https://www.cm-labs.com/news/vortex-studio-2019b-release/)). AGX Dynamics for Unreal splits one large landscape into simulated terrain tiles: **"only the terrain areas that are close to the active object are loaded into working memory"**, and the rest are paged to disk ([Algoryx blog](https://algoryx.se/introducing-data-plotting-and-paging-terrain-in-agx-dynamics-for-unreal-engine)).

Games arrive at the same architecture under tighter budgets. Spintires:MudRunner cuts its 1 km maps into **16 m blocks**. Every block carries a coarse 25x25 base texture (elevation, a two-material mix, a softness tint, a mud mask). A **128x128 mud layer (12.5 cm cells) is allocated only on blocks that contain mud**, holding mud height over base terrain plus a lateral offset for berms. Only blocks near the truck are simulated ([Zagrebelnyy, Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/programming/mud-and-water-of-spintires-mudrunner)). Artists paint the mud with a brush. The physics shipped to the Nintendo Switch unchanged while the graphics were cut, which shows how cheap a sparse 2.5D layer is next to rendering ([80.lv](https://80.lv/articles/mudrunner-porting-a-successful-title-to-console/?amp=1)). Batman: Arkham Origins adds the resource-management half of the pattern. A pool of snow heightmaps is allocated and recycled by proximity and visibility, **only two surfaces update per frame**, a small per-update subtraction lets snow refill, and the total cost is **about 2-4 MB and under 1 ms of GPU time** ([GDC 2014 slides](https://www.slideshare.net/colinbb/gdc2014-deformable-snow-rendering-in-batman-arkham-origins)).

| System | Where soil exists | What is active | State storage |
|---|---|---|---|
| Chrono SCM | SCM patch, optional `SetBoundary` AABB | OOBB per wheel body | Sparse grows-only node map, 16x16 node tiles |
| Chrono CRM | Particle bed, optional moving patch | Box around each body | Inactive particles freed from GPU |
| Vortex | Authored Earthwork Zone / Deformable Terrain | Zone | Not published |
| AGX for Unreal | Paged terrain tiles | Tiles near active objects | Paged to disk |
| Spintires | Painted mud blocks (16 m) | Blocks near the truck | 128x128 offset texture over base |
| Batman AO (visual) | Snow surfaces | Pool by proximity, 2 per frame | Recycled heightmaps with refill |

The step from rendering to physics is smaller than it looks. NVIDIA published a wheel-rut algorithm for PhysX heightfields, inspired by Spintires. It converts each wheel's footprint into a sample rectangle and pushes the samples down to the wheel surface, scaled by load, normal speed and slip. A compaction floor clamps the depth, the displaced volume is redistributed to neighbors as berms, and only that sub-rectangle is written back through `PxHeightField::modifySamples`. Fast wheels are swept between poses so they leave no holes ([NVIDIA](https://developer.nvidia.com/content/physx-dynamic-heightfield-modifications)). The order of operations carries over directly to our design. Its displacement law is empirical, though, and we want a terramechanics one in its place.

## Real-time fidelity tops out at Bekker-Wong-Janosi, and mud breaks it

The fidelity ladder is well mapped, and only its lower rungs run in real time. Chrono's CRM is the best-documented GPU continuum model (SPH with μ(I) rheology). Even after active domains, its **real-time factor was 20.25 for a single MGRU3 wheel and 70.49 for the RASSOR drum on a 16 GB RTX 4080-class GPU**, at step sizes of 5e-5 to 5e-4 s ([Hu et al., arXiv 2507.05643](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.05643v2)). Its headline "10 km, 100 M particles at near-interactive rates" refers to that moving active box, not to a full car at rally speed. Chrono's own lunar-sensor paper concedes that terramechanics-heavy cases do not run in real time ([arXiv 2410.04371](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.04371)). DEM is **2-3 orders of magnitude slower than SCM** ([arXiv 2407.18903](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.18903)). NVIDIA's new mixed MPM, the solver behind Newton's experimental granular module, ran **49 M sand particles at about 4 s per frame** and showed no wheeled vehicle ([NVIDIA PRL](https://research.nvidia.com/labs/prl/mixed_mpm/)). A faster RTX 5090 does not close a gap of one to two orders of magnitude.

That leaves the semi-empirical family, and within it SCM's formulation is the one that is both real-time and history-aware. Per node, SCM computes an elastic trial stress, then Bekker-Wong pressure-sinkage, **p = (Kc/b + Kφ)zⁿ**, once the node's historical yield stress is exceeded. It adds a vertical damping term, caps shear with Mohr-Coulomb, and grows shear with Janosi-Hanamoto, **τ = (c + p tan φ)(1 − e^(−j/K))**, from slip accumulated at that node. Because each node remembers its yield stress, a second pass over the same spot reacts elastically until the old pressure is exceeded, so multipass compaction falls out of the model ([SCMTerrain.cpp](https://github.com/projectchrono/chrono/blob/main/src/chrono_vehicle/terrain/SCMTerrain.cpp)). Bulldozing is a heuristic: displaced volume is moved to the rim of the contact patch and smoothed by an erosion pass limited by an angle of repose. Chrono's authors describe SCM as **"real-time or close"** and **about 10x faster than CRM** ([ECCOMAS 2023](https://multibody2023.tecnico.ulisboa.pt/prog_MULTIBODY_WEB/MULTBODY2023_ABSTRACTS/ID_106_121_extendedAbstract.pdf)). The docs also state that **"handling tires cannot be used"**: SCM wants a mesh tire, not a raycast wheel like PhysX's ([Chrono terrain docs](https://api.projectchrono.org/vehicle_terrain.html)). Chrono therefore cannot be dropped into our PhysX loop. The node algorithm is about forty lines, though, and fits a GPU kernel over the nodes under each tire.

There is a cheaper rung still. A closed-form Wong-Reece rigid-wheel kernel integrates normal and shear stress over the contact arc and costs a **median 0.50 ms per wheel evaluation with 100 arc points** ([arXiv 2606.21755](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.21755)). It needs no grid, but it produces no ruts. Cheaper again, cone-index mobility-number models (Brixius, adopted in ASAE D497) map one dimensionless number to net traction and motion resistance ([arXiv 2604.17991](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.17991)). Bekker parameters can be predicted from cone-index data ([ISTVS](https://www.istvs.org/publication-news/predicting-terrain-parameters-for-physics-based-vehicle-mobility-models-from-cone-index-data)), which gives the rigid layer and the zone layer one parameter source. Commercial vehicle-dynamics tire-soil models (Altair's Soft Soil Tire Model, FTire/soil, CarSim) all sit on the same Bekker/Mohr-Coulomb/Janosi base. On top they add a slip-angle-dependent lateral shear modulus and a cap on tire-soil friction separate from soil shear. Tire flexibility enters as a "larger substitute circle" iterated until the soil reaction balances tire deflection ([Altair docs](https://help.altair.com/hwdesktop/hwx/topics/motionview/soft_soil_tire_r.htm)). That iteration is a cheap way to give a raycast wheel a pneumatic carcass.

Accuracy should be judged honestly. Bekker-Wong models match single-wheel drawbar pull to **±20-30% per point**. A rigid-wheel kernel showed a **52% median sinkage error** in its calibrated regime, which grew to **135% (drawbar pull) and 385% (sinkage)** on a firm soil outside it ([arXiv 2606.21755](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.21755)). SCM is known to struggle at high slip and with soil flow ([arXiv 2507.05643](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.05643v2)), and those are exactly the rally regimes: wheelspin, dig-in, roost. Published parameter tables also disagree by orders of magnitude, because Kc and Kφ are plate-size-dependent fits. A CRM-calibrated simulant even came out with a **negative Kc** ([ECCOMAS 2023](https://multibody2023.tecnico.ulisboa.pt/prog_MULTIBODY_WEB/MULTBODY2023_ABSTRACTS/ID_106_121_extendedAbstract.pdf)). Parameters therefore have to be calibrated against a target behavior, not copied from a table.

Mud is the weakest point of every family. "No accurate mud model currently accounts for pore water in fine-grained clays", and rapid tire shear can liquefy wet clay toward hydroplaning-like behavior; the state of the art is a research SPH-FEA project with no real-time variant ([VT/GVSC project](https://arc.engin.umich.edu/research/projects/tiremud-interaction-modeled-using-smoothed-particle-hydrodynamics-and-finite-element)). Bekker parameters for clay loam swing strongly with moisture and loading speed ([Int. Agrophysics 2024](https://www.international-agrophysics.org/pdf-187017-110858?controller=index&action=magazine&magSubpage=pdf&articleId=187017&pdfId=110858&filename=Evaluation+of+the+changes.pdf)). The only rate-dependent term in SCM is its damping R. A real-time mud model will therefore be engineered, not validated. The engineering is SCM with low φ and cohesion standing in for undrained strength, a slip-rate strength reduction, damping, and a tire-soil friction cap that falls with water content. Games reach the same place from the other side. Spintires' developer calls his wheel-mud forces "pure empirics" ([Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/programming/mud-and-water-of-spintires-mudrunner)). BeamNG models mud as a parametric resistive volume, with no geometry change: a sink depth plus power-law fluid drag (`fluidDensity`, `flowConsistencyIndex`, `flowBehaviorIndex`) on top of Stribeck friction, assigned per physics material ([BeamNG ground models](https://docs.beamng.com/modding/levels/level_formats/groundmodels/)).

## Outside zones, a parametric surface layer carries wet, gravel and road evolution

Most of a km-scale map never deforms, and good practice there is a friction model indexed by surface state. In practice, surface condition enters a tire model as a peak-friction scale, such as LMUX/LMUY in the Magic Formula ([MathWorks](https://www.mathworks.com/help/sdl/ref/tireroadinteractionmagicformula.html)). In PhysX it is the per-(tire, material) value in `PhysxVehicleTireFrictionTable`: one friction value per ground physics material, a default for unlisted ones, and a three-point `frictionVsSlipGraph` that shapes the curve ([Omniverse schema](https://docs.omniverse.nvidia.com/kit/docs/omni_usd_schema_physics/latest/class_physx_schema_physx_vehicle_tire_friction_table.html)). Starting values are well established: **dry asphalt 0.80-0.90 peak / 0.75 sliding, wet asphalt 0.50-0.70 / 0.45-0.60, gravel 0.60 / 0.55, dry earth 0.68 / 0.65, wet earth 0.55 / 0.40-0.50, packed snow 0.20 / 0.15, ice 0.10 / 0.07** (Wong, via [hpwizard](https://hpwizard.com/tire-friction-coefficient.html)). Scaling alone is right for wet asphalt and wrong for loose surfaces. Burckhardt fits show snow with a much sharper initial rise than asphalt (c2 = 94 against 24) ([arXiv 2211.10336](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.10336)). On gravel, gravel-on-gravel shear limits friction, so peak and sliding are nearly equal, and lateral force curves on wet gravel and pea stone have shapes of their own ([SAE 2010-01-0374](https://saemobilus.sae.org/content/2010-01-0374)). This matters for our ABS and TC: carmakers warn that **ABS can lengthen stopping distance on loose surfaces** ([Kia manual](https://ownersmanual.kia.com/full_webhelp/MV1/2024/en_US/topics/t00454.html)), and a falling asphalt-shaped curve on gravel would hide that effect from the controller.

Water needs a depth, not just a "wet" flag. Friction loss is **steepest in the first millimeter of water film** ([IJTST 2024](https://doaj.org/article/40e6954906944a4c8b92d948586a26bc)). Each pavement has a "critical water thickness" that depends on macrotexture, microtexture and speed ([HAL](https://hal.archives-ouvertes.fr/hal-00906426)). Bosch gives **0.55-0.65 at 0.2 mm of water with new tread but 0.20-0.50 at 1.6 mm tread**, and 0-0.55 in heavy rain or puddles ([hpwizard citing Bosch](https://hpwizard.com/tire-friction-coefficient.html)), so tread depth is part of the wet state. Horne's full-hydroplaning onset, **V(mph) = 10.35·√p(psi)**, ignores water depth and is not conservative for radial tires ([TTI 147-1](https://library.ctr.utexas.edu/hostedPDFs/tti/147-1.pdf); [Pilot Institute](https://pilotinstitute.com/aircraft-hydroplaning/)). At 33-36 psi it gives roughly 95-100 km/h, an upper bound. Industry tire models now take water depth and speed as inputs: Simcenter Tire 2512 added a wet-road MF-Tyre/MF-Swift model ([Siemens](https://blogs.sw.siemens.com/simcenter/?p=71656)), and Kunos simulates an actual water film in ACC ([forum quote](https://forums.overclockers.co.uk/posts/32154148)). Because a PhysX friction value is static per material, the speed and depth dependence has to be applied from outside, each step, per wheel.

Rally titles add the last ingredient: surface evolution kept as cheap per-region state rather than geometry. Richard Burns Rally gives "each texture its own material map" with gravel depths and states, and early cars sweep loose material for later ones ([RBR interview](https://bhms.racesimcentral.net/print/150)). DiRT Rally 2.0 runs gravel degradation through **150 steps**, from swept line to ploughed ruts with changed camber, and lets tarmac "rubber in" ([GTPlanet](https://www.gtplanet.net/dirt-rally-2-0-track-surfaces-will-degrade-with-every-run/); [Team VVV](https://www.teamvvv.com/?p=50044)). Forza Horizon 5's rally expansion lays "pre-deformed tire tracks" under its runtime deformation ([VGC](https://videogameschronicle.com/news/forza-horizon-5s-second-expansion-rally-adventure-has-been-revealed)), so authored ruts can give rut-steering wherever no live soil is simulated. No primary source says whether any of these titles feed ruts into physics geometry.

The reference stack offers little here. **CARLA's weather is visual only.** Wetness "only affects the RGB camera sensor" and weather affects neither "the actor's physics nor other sensors" ([CARLA Python API](https://carla.readthedocs.io/en/0.9.16/python_api/)). CARLA's spatial friction is a run-time box trigger (`static.trigger.friction`) ([CARLA docs](https://carla.readthedocs.io/en/0.9.16/tuto_G_add_friction_triggers)). It appears to scale friction for the whole vehicle on overlap, which would rule out mu-split ([CarlaWheeledVehicle.cpp](https://carla.org/Doxygen/html/dc/d65/CarlaWheeledVehicle_8cpp_source.html)); the source should be checked before relying on either reading. CARLA does parse OpenDRIVE lane `<material surface friction roughness>` ([LaneParser.cpp](https://carla.org/Doxygen/html/d0/d2e/LaneParser_8cpp_source.html)), and industrial tools use OpenCRG grids with friction maps ([arXiv 2609.12795](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.12795)). Both are a natural import path for a raster. Transitions are a known trap: PhysX takes **one material per wheel query** and CARLA triggers switch on overlap, so every surface edge is a mu step unless the integrator blends it ([Omniverse schema](https://docs.omniverse.nvidia.com/kit/docs/omni_usd_schema_physics/latest/class_physx_schema_physx_vehicle_tire_friction_table.html)). Blending is needed to keep ABS and TC from chattering at edges. A puddle under one side is a different case: a deliberate mu-split that should stay per wheel.

## Isaac Sim 6.1 supplies friction tables and component seams, not soft terrain

Our backend gives the rigid layer for free and the zone layer not at all. Isaac Sim 6.1 updated its experimental Newton integration to 1.5.0 (adding the VBD and XPBD solvers and hydroelastic contacts) and moved omni.physx and omni.physx.vehicle to 110.3.2. Its release notes mention no deformables, particles, MPM or heightfield work ([Isaac Sim 6.1 release notes](https://docs.isaacsim.omniverse.nvidia.com/6.1.0/overview/release_notes.html)). Newton's implicit MPM is documented in Isaac Lab, under an Isaac Sim 6.2 badge, as **experimental**, with rigid–MPM coupling through an MJWarp proxy and an example grid box of **0.2 x 0.2 x 0.2 m** ([Isaac Lab MPM](https://isaac-sim.github.io/IsaacLab/develop/source/concepts/using_mpm.html)). It does not couple to the PhysX vehicle and has no vehicle example ([Newton examples](https://github.com/newton-physics/newton/tree/main/newton/examples/mpm)). At most it could serve a decoupled showcase sand pit.

The seams that matter sit in PhysX itself. Vehicle2 is a chain of `PxVehicleComponent`s, and the docs say the road-geometry component **"may be replaced by any component that can compute a plane and a friction value for each wheel"**; the tire component can likewise be replaced by any model that consumes slip, load and friction ([PhysX 5.4.1 Vehicles](https://nvidia-omniverse.github.io/PhysX/physx/5.4.1/docs/Vehicles.html)). That pair is the soft-soil hook. The road query returns a plane lowered by the computed sinkage and the effective friction, and the tire stage adds Bekker resistance and Janosi-limited traction. I found no documentation saying whether omni.physx.vehicle 110.3.x exposes custom components, a tire-force callback or per-wheel friction overrides to Kit extensions; this is the most important unknown. I also found no coupling of Chrono to PhysX, Isaac or Unreal, so we should assume none exists. Chrono's own co-simulation framework still shows the shape of the coupling. It is a "3-way explicit force-displacement" exchange in which the multibody node sends spindle states and receives forces, with a rigid-body `BODY` interface for each wheel ([Chrono cosim](https://api.chrono.projectchrono.org/group__vehicle__cosim.html)). Collapsed into one process, that exchange is our wheel-soil coupling.

## Design recommendations

### Build two layers fed by one surface-state raster

The world-wide layer should be a georeferenced raster, about 0.5-1 m cells, tiled to match the map. Each cell holds a surface class (asphalt, concrete, gravel, dirt, grass, sand, mud, snow, ice), water depth h in mm, wetness w from 0 to 1, a loose-material or degradation state (8 bits, DR2-style), and a flag naming the deformable zone the cell belongs to, if any. Seed the classes from the CARLA town's existing materials and semantics, and from OpenDRIVE lane materials where present. Paint off-road classes and zone polygons on top for the test field. Store the per-class parameters in a BeamNG-style table keyed by material name: friction peak and slide, slip-curve points, rolling-resistance coefficient, and Bekker/Janosi parameters derived from cone index. That keeps tuning out of the geometry. On the Isaac side, give the exported town meshes and test-field tiles USD physics materials per class and a friction table per tire type. Mu-split then comes from PhysX's per-wheel material query at no extra cost. Do not add CARLA-style trigger boxes. Hold the raster in the world server next to the weather, and re-apply it on every map load and reconnect, as the bridge already does for weather. CARLA's visual puddles and wetness then become one consumer of that state, not a separate source of truth.

Per wheel and per substep, the rigid layer computes mu_eff = mu_class(w) · f_hp(v / V_hp(h, tread, p)), where f_hp falls smoothly toward about 0.05-0.1 as speed approaches a Horne-derived, depth-reduced onset. It writes that value as the wheel's friction. It blends the scalar inputs bilinearly but keeps the class nearest-neighbor, and applies a short first-order lag in distance, so asphalt-to-gravel edges do not jolt ABS or TC; puddle edges stay sharp, per wheel. Gravel and loose snow get flat or rising post-peak slip graphs, asphalt gets a falling one, and ice and snow get the sharp early peak. The ego's ABS and TC slip targets should be reviewed per surface against those curves. Gravel lines evolve through the degradation state, a cheap non-geometric stand-in for "the 10th car finds a cleaner line", and authored rut height detail can be baked into the test-field terrain for rut-steering outside live zones.

### Couple soil to PhysX at the wheel, inside the substep

Inside a zone, each wheel runs an SCM-style node loop over the sparse grid cells under its footprint. It takes wheel pose, spin and slip from PhysX, computes the elastic or plastic Bekker pressure (b from patch area and perimeter) and the Janosi shear, writes back node sinkage and yield stress, and returns sinkage, normal-force correction, traction cap and rolling-resistance torque. Add an Altair-style substitute-circle iteration so pneumatic deflection on firmer soil is not treated as a rigid wheel. If vehicle2 components are reachable, return the lowered plane and effective friction through a replacement road-geometry component. If they are not, use the script path: shift the wheel's effective contact plane (or suspension reference) by the sinkage, set the wheel's friction to the soil-limited value, and apply the resistance as an external wheel torque or chassis force. The exchange is explicit with a one-substep lag, which is what Chrono's co-simulation uses. Soft soil is compliant enough for that to be stable at substep rates. The rate is set by speed: a car at 25 m/s crosses a 5 cm cell in 2 ms, so zone wheels need **~500 Hz or more**, or an analytic integration along the patch, and the coupling cannot cross the 20-50 Hz lockstep frame boundary or a process boundary. Over a band one to two wheel diameters wide at the zone edge, blend rigid-layer and soil forces by distance. Initialize every node from the rigid surface height so entry has no drop. Keep a Wong-Reece closed-form wheel as a no-rut fallback for zones far from the ego, and as a reference to check the grid model against.

Mud should be treated explicitly as an engineered model. Start from low φ with cohesion standing in for undrained strength, nonzero damping R, a slip-rate softening factor, and a tire-soil friction cap that falls with water content. Add a per-tire "clogging" state that lowers the traction cap after sustained slip in mud and recovers on firm ground. Document all of it as unvalidated.

### Persist and stream deltas, never terrain

Store soil state as sparse node blocks (SCM uses 16x16 nodes) keyed by world tile, allocated the first time a wheel enters, over immutable base terrain. At 4 cm spacing and roughly 8 bytes per node (height delta, plastic sinkage, yield stress and shear accumulation at fp16), four 0.5 m-wide tracks over a kilometer touch about 1.25 M nodes, or **~10 MB per km driven**. Whole-zone dense allocation is unnecessary even on a large test field. Run the soil kernel only for blocks inside the active boxes around each vehicle's wheels, as Chrono, AGX and Spintires do. Freeze blocks that leave a radius R. Write dirty blocks to disk per session, Chrono-style (`GetModifiedNodes`/`SetModifiedNodes`) or AGX-style paging, so a scenario can start fresh, carry ruts over from a previous run, or load a baked "10th car" rut state. A decay or refill term, as in Batman's refill, is optional and per class: it suits snow and wet sand, not clay. Two things matter for repeatability. Stamping must happen in a fixed per-vehicle, per-wheel order inside the lockstep tick, so replays and multi-AV runs are deterministic. And every vehicle, NPC or ego, must read and write the same grid, so ruts left by one are felt by the others; SynChrono does this across ranks for SCM ([SBEL 2020](https://sbel.wisc.edu/2020/07/23/synchrono-moves-off-road/)).

### Make sensors and physics read the same state

RTX lidar and cameras ray-trace render geometry, so ruts reach Autoware's perception only if the render mesh changes. For dirty blocks only, update a per-tile mesh or displacement at 5-10 Hz, under or behind the wheels. Leave PhysX collision on the original surface and let the wheel model carry sinkage analytically. That is the "visual mesh updated, collision not" split that games and rover simulators use ([lunar rover study review](https://themoonlight.io/review/modeling-of-terrain-deformation-by-a-grouser-wheel-for-lunar-rover-simulation)), and it avoids re-cooking collision at run time. NPC wheels still need the soil model, or they will float over ruts the ego's lidar can see. Wet state must reach sensors from the same raster. Wet roads reflect lidar pulses specularly away and raise no-return counts ([US 11828854](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/11828854)), rain lowers point count and intensity ([Sensors 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10051412)), and spray produces false points ([IEEE RA-L 2022](https://unpaywall.org/10.1109%2FLRA.2022.3148443)). A first model drops ground returns with probability p(w, incidence), scales intensity by (1 − k·w), and injects spray behind wheels when h > 0 at speed. Every renderer, lidar and friction parameter should be derived from the same (class, w, h, degradation, delta-height) state, so no subsystem can disagree about where the puddle or the rut is.

### Calibrate with virtual bevameters and behavioral targets

Per soil class, fit the zone model's parameters to behavior, not to a published table. Run CRM, or DEM where needed, offline as a virtual bevameter: plate sinkage and annular shear tests, then a single-wheel drawbar-pull-versus-slip sweep at our wheel load, width and diameter. Fit the SCM parameters by Bayesian inference, as SBEL did ([ECCOMAS 2023](https://multibody2023.tecnico.ulisboa.pt/prog_MULTIBODY_WEB/MULTBODY2023_ABSTRACTS/ID_106_121_extendedAbstract.pdf); [arXiv 2407.18903](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.18903)). Where the off-road field can be measured, cone-index readings anchor both layers through the CI-to-Bekker mapping. Judge success by trends within ±20-30%. Sinkage should grow with slip and load. A second pass should sink less. Lateral grip should fall in sand. Rolling resistance should outgrow thrust as sinkage deepens, as observed on loose sand ([Cranfield](https://dspace.lib.cranfield.ac.uk/handle/1826/13691)). Validate the rigid layer with the ABS/TC scenario set the evidence points to: dry-to-wet step, one-side puddle (mu-split), gravel braking (the ABS penalty), an ice patch, speed rising toward hydroplaning, and zone entry at speed (the force-blend band).

## Conclusion

The question is less about which soil solver to pick than about where state lives. Once soil is a sparse delta over immutable terrain, read through a single raster, map size stops mattering: cost follows the driven corridor (about 10 MB per km) and the number of wheels in contact, not the town. The engineering risk then concentrates in places a whole-world solver would have hidden. One is a single integration seam, how soil forces reach the PhysX wheel at substep rate in omni.physx.vehicle. The other is a single physics gap, mud, which no published model handles. Both can be checked cheaply before any soil code exists, and they should be the first work items.

The architecture also reframes "high fidelity". Most of what a rally ego and its ABS/TC feel comes from the rigid layer: water depth, gravel slip-curve shape, mu-split, transitions. The parametric game techniques (material maps, degradation states, baked ruts) handle that layer better than continuum mechanics would. The zone model adds what the rigid layer cannot provide: memory, meaning ruts, compaction and sinkage that grows with slip. The best test of the system is therefore its second pass. The second car through the mud should meet a different surface from the first, and the lidar should see the same ruts the wheels feel.
